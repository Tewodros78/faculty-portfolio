Research Statement

My research asks how large, covariate shocks (armed conflict, climate shocks, and commodity price shocks) affect food security and household welfare in low-income countries, and what that implies for humanitarian and development policy. I focus on Ethiopia, where recurring conflict, drought, and inflation interact, and I use modern causal inference methods to separate the effects of these shocks.

Current work: conflict and food insecurity

My job market paper and dissertation estimate the effect of conflict escalation on acute food insecurity. Using a monthly panel of 1,848 region-month observations from January 2010 to December 2023 and a dynamic difference-in-differences design, I find that regions experiencing significant conflict escalation saw food insecurity rise by about 0.61 IPC phase units relative to stable regions. The effect peaked during the 2021–2022 civil war and declined after the November 2022 Pretoria Cessation of Hostilities Agreement, but it remained elevated through late 2023. A related working paper extends this analysis to the joint role of conflict and climate shocks using a dynamic event-study approach.

Price shocks and macroeconomic vulnerability

A second line of work studies how global shocks reach households through prices. In a 2026 paper, I use machine learning methods to show that oil price growth, lagged domestic inflation, and global inflation are the strongest predictors of inflation in Ethiopia. Earlier work examined how the predictability of foreign aid affects economic growth in Ethiopia.

Inequality and health policy

At Howard's Center for Equitable Economy and Sustainable Society, I study racial inequality in household balance sheets in the United States (2007–2022) and the classification and effectiveness of Prescription Drug Monitoring Programs. This work broadens my methods and connects my interest in shocks and vulnerability to policy questions in the United States.

Future agenda

Next, I plan to move from regional to household-level evidence by linking geocoded conflict events and rainfall data to household panel surveys. This will let me study which households are most exposed, how they cope (through consumption, assets, labor, and migration), and whether social protection programs soften the impact of shocks. The grant concept below describes the first project in this agenda.

Dissertation

Conflict Escalation and Acute Food Insecurity in Ethiopia: Difference-in-Differences Analysis
Dissertation · Department of Economics, Howard University · Advisor: Dr. Elizabeth Asiedu
This dissertation estimates the causal effect of conflict escalation on acute food insecurity in Ethiopia. It builds a monthly regional panel (January 2010 – December 2023; 1,848 region-month observations) that links conflict event data with Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) outcomes, and it uses a dynamic difference-in-differences design. Regions with significant conflict escalation experienced an increase of about 0.61 IPC phase units in food insecurity relative to stable regions. The effect peaked during the 2021–2022 civil war, fell after the Pretoria Cessation of Hostilities Agreement, and remained elevated through 2023. The findings point to the persistence of conflict's effects after fighting ends and to the need for sustained humanitarian and recovery support.

Grant Concept Proposal

A concept proposal for the first household-level project in my research agenda.

Title

Shocks on Top of Shocks: Conflict, Drought, and Household Food Security in Ethiopia

Target funder

International Growth Centre (IGC) Ethiopia research funding, or an equivalent small research grant for early-career development economists.

Problem and significance

Ethiopia has faced civil war, repeated drought, and high inflation at the same time. My dissertation shows that conflict escalation sharply raised acute food insecurity at the regional level and that the effect persisted after the 2022 peace agreement. Regional averages, however, hide which households suffer most and how they cope. Policy makers need household-level evidence to target food aid and social protection when shocks overlap.

Research questions

(1) How do conflict exposure and drought, separately and together, affect household food consumption and dietary diversity? (2) Which households are most vulnerable (by livelihood, assets, gender of household head, and location)? (3) Does participation in Ethiopia's Productive Safety Net Programme (PSNP) reduce the effect of these shocks?

Data and methods

I will link the Ethiopia Socioeconomic Survey household panel (World Bank LSMS-ISA) to geocoded conflict events (ACLED) and satellite rainfall data (CHIRPS) at the community level. Using household fixed effects and event-study difference-in-differences designs, I will estimate the effects of conflict and drought exposure, test whether they compound, and examine heterogeneity by PSNP participation. Robustness checks will include alternative exposure definitions, estimators robust to staggered treatment timing, and placebo tests.
