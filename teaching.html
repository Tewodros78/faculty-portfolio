Teaching Philosophy

I teach economics as a practical framework for real-world questions. At Salale University, many students came from rural households facing the price shocks, droughts, and labor-market constraints we studied. Economics became meaningful when they saw their communities reflected in the models and data. This experience shapes three teaching commitments.

1. Learning by doing with real data

Students deepen their understanding of econometrics and economic theory through practice. In my courses, they use Stata, R, or Python to clean real-world data, estimate models, and interpret results. For research methods and data analysis, I design assignments that guide students from developing a research question to writing a short paper, giving them a full-cycle experience.

2. Clear structure and high expectations for every student

I hold all students to high standards and give them the structure to meet them. As a recitation instructor in the American Economic Association Summer Program, I worked with students preparing for graduate study in advanced microeconomics. I learned to break difficult proofs and problems into steps, to check understanding frequently, and to make it normal to ask questions. I use worked examples, practice problems that increase in difficulty, and short low-stakes checks so that students who arrive with different preparation can all succeed. At Howard, I am especially committed to supporting first-generation students and students from groups that are underrepresented in economics.

3. Assessment that supports learning

I use assessment to give students feedback, not only grades. I combine problem sets, applied data assignments, and exams with written feedback, and I use mid-semester check-ins to adjust my teaching. Having advised about six senior essays and theses each year at Salale University, I have seen how timely, specific feedback helps students grow into independent researchers.

My goal is for students to leave my courses able to think carefully about evidence, to use data responsibly, and to explain economic ideas to people outside the classroom.
